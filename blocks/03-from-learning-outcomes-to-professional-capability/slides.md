  Narration (0:00-0:45):
  Two modules can publish very similar learning outcomes and still produce learners with very different professional capabilities. That may be manageable inside a single course or institution. notes: |
  Narration (0:45-2:15):
  The document is clear that learning outcomes and competency statements are complementary, not competing alternatives.

  A learning outcome describes what a learner is expected to know or be able to demonstrate at the end of a specific module. It is an internal organising unit for teaching and assessment.

  A competency statement describes what the learner can do in a real working context, at a recognised EQF level and with reference to an external classification. It is the module's public interface to other modules, providers, employers and credential systems.

  Source: Section 5.3.4 and Appendix A.1.

title: Why learning outcomes alone do not stack
full:
  type: ul
  items:
    - They do not state the competencies a learner must already possess
    - They do not declare the professional capability produced by completion
    - They do not necessarily connect to a shared external classification
    - Similar outcomes can lead to materially different competencies
    - Another provider cannot reliably test whether one module prepares a learner for the next
notes: |
  Narration (2:15-3:30):
  Consider the outcome: “The learner will be able to explain the principles of HACCP.” It says something useful about the module, but very little about the surrounding pathway.

  It does not identify what the learner must already know. It does not say what the learner can do next. It does not connect the result to a classification that another provider, employer or qualification authority can interpret consistently.

  Learning outcomes remain necessary. The document's argument is that they are insufficient as the principal organising unit for a stackable and portable microcredential architecture.

  Source: Sections 5.3 and 5.3.1.

card2:

    On what is the action performed?

    HACCP plan · treatment regimen · herd-health protocol
head3: Context
card3:
  type: p
  text: |-
    In what working environment?

    On a processing line · under farm SOPs · during emergency response
head4: Standard
card4:
  type: p
  text: |-
    How is performance judged?

    Against legislation · to a rubric · with mentor sign-off · with auditable records
notes: |
  Narration (3:30-5:15):
  Appendix A identifies four elements in a well-formed competency statement.

  The action verb names what the learner does. The object names the thing acted upon. The context defines the working environment in which the action is performed. The standard explains how performance will be judged.

  These elements are written as continuous prose rather than as a checklist. Nevertheless, each should be recognisable. A statement such as “understand antimicrobial resistance” is not externally verifiable. A statement such as “apply HACCP” remains too generic because the object, context and standard are missing.

  Source: Appendix A.2 and Appendix A.7.

--- slide
id: s02-06
layout: Comparison
title: Level is not contained in the verb
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**EQF Levels**"
    - "Apply an established protocol in a routine setting: EQF 3–4"
    - "Manage variation, supervise others or develop practice: EQF 5"
    - "Decide under uncertainty or lead new practice: EQF 6–8"
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - DOK concerns the cognitive demand of the whole task
    - "Routine application with stable parameters: commonly DOK 2"
    - "Incomplete information, competing constraints or sustained synthesis: DOK 3–4"
notes: |
  Narration (6:45-8:15):
  Competency level cannot be assigned from the action verb alone. The responsibility-and-autonomy strand is particularly important when anchoring a statement to the EQF.

  Applying an established protocol in a routine context may sit at EQF three or four. Managing variation or supervising others may move the task to EQF five. Taking responsibility for decisions under uncertainty or leading the development of new practice moves the capability towards EQF six to eight.

  Webb's Depth of Knowledge provides a complementary check. Applying a procedure under stable conditions and applying it under incomplete information may use the same verb, but they impose different cognitive demands. The object, context and performance standard determine the depth.

  Source: Section 3.1 and Appendix A.4–A.4.1.

id: s02-07
layout: Comparison
title: Which statement should lead the design?
left_head: Lead with the competency when…
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - The module contributes to a defined occupational or professional role
    - It must stack or interoperate across providers
    - Employers or regulators are a principal audience
    - Assessment is mainly performance- or workplace-based
right_head: Lead with the learning outcome when…
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - The module is standalone or institutionally self-contained
    - Learners choosing courses are the principal audience
    - Content is knowledge-rich and foundational
    - Assessment centres on knowledge and comprehension
    - No settled external competency framework exists
notes: |
  Narration (8:15-10:00):
  Both statements are required. The practical decision is which one should be written first and used as the anchor.

  Competency-led design is appropriate when the module serves a recognised occupational role, must stack across providers, speaks to employers or regulators, or relies on workplace performance evidence. The competency is identified first and module learning outcomes are then derived from it.

  Outcome-led design may be appropriate for standalone, foundational or knowledge-rich provision, particularly where no settled external competency framework exists. Once the outcomes are established, they are mapped to the nearest appropriate ESCO or sector-defined competency. The resulting claim must then be reviewed against the assessment evidence.

  The document explicitly cautions against applying this guidance rigidly.

  Source: Section 5.3.4.

notes: |
  Narration (10:00-11:30):
  The document specifies four declarations that every microcredential should publish.

  Prerequisite competencies define the entry interface. Outgoing competencies define what the credential claims and the evidence supporting those claims. Institutional learning outcomes remain inside that competency interface as the basis for pedagogical and assessment design. Workload or duration is then stated in a recognised unit.

  Publishing this interface makes the pathway transparent. One provider can test whether a learner's outgoing competencies satisfy another module's prerequisites. A different provider may substitute its module if it declares the same outgoing capability. This is the basis of a genuinely federated system.

  Source: Sections 5.3.3 and the four-interface-declarations design summary.

title: Work backwards from the destination
left:
  type: ol
  items:
    - Identify the output competencies required at the learner's destination
    - Trace backwards through the prerequisite chain
    - Separate the common core from specialist branch modules
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**A genuinely different pathway changes at least one:**"
    - Role destination
    - Competency emphasis
    - Assessment context
    - Credential type
notes: |
  Narration (11:30-13:00):
  Stackable design changes the curriculum question. Instead of asking only what the course contains, the designer asks what journeys are possible and where each journey ends.

  For each learner profile, identify the output competencies required at the destination. Trace backwards through the prerequisite chain to determine which modules are needed. This reveals the common core and the specialist branches.

  Pathways that differ only in speed or depth are not genuinely distinct. The document identifies four meaningful dimensions of difference: role destination, competency emphasis, assessment context and credential type. If the difference between two pathways cannot be articulated as a different capability, the pathways are duplicative rather than stackable.

  Source: Appendix B.3–B.3.3.

id: s02-10
layout: Full
title: Apply the design test
full:
  type: ol
  items:
    - Select one learning outcome from a module you teach
    - Name the professional capability to which it contributes
    - Draft the action, object, context and performance standard
    - Anchor the claim to an appropriate EQF level
    - Decide whether the competency or the learning outcome should lead
    - Check whether the planned assessment can support the competency claim
notes: |
  Narration (13:00-15:00):
  Apply the document's design logic to one existing learning outcome. Do not attempt to redesign an entire programme.

  Identify the professional capability to which the outcome contributes. Draft a competency statement containing an observable action, the object acted upon, the working context and the standard of performance. Then consider the responsibility, autonomy and cognitive demand required and anchor the statement to an appropriate EQF level.

  Decide consciously which statement should lead this design. Finally, review the proposed assessment. A declaration of workplace capability cannot be supported by a recall test alone. The evidence must be proportionate to the claim.

  Pause the presentation while you draft. The resulting statement can be reviewed against the fuller guidance and worked examples in Appendix A.

  Source: Sections 5.3.4–5.3.5 and Appendices A.2, A.4 and A.7.
