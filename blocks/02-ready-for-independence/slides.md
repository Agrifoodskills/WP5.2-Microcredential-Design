  Narration (0:45-2:15):
  The document begins with three dominant paradigms of learning. Behaviourism focuses on observable changes in behaviour, shaped through interaction with the environment and reinforcement. Cognitivism focuses on internal processes such as memory, perception, problem-solving and metacognition. Constructivism holds that learners actively build understanding through experience and reflection, rather than passively receiving knowledge.

  Each paradigm suggests different training methods. Behaviourism supports instruction, practice and feedback. Cognitivism supports organised information, analogy and active mental processing. Constructivism supports exploration, collaborative work and application to real situations.

  The document does not select one paradigm and reject the others. It argues that they are complementary in practice. Memorising a fact or procedure may benefit from behaviourist methods. Deeper understanding requires cognitive strategies. Complex problem-solving and transfer to unfamiliar situations draw strongly on constructivist approaches, particularly where adult learners bring substantial prior experience.

title: Pedagogy and andragogy are not rigid age categories
subtitle: They are frameworks for approaching learning and instruction across a mixed learner population.
notes: |
  Narration (3:15-4:30):
  Pedagogy is commonly associated with teaching children and andragogy with teaching adults. The document cautions against treating them as rigid age categories. They are frameworks that help educators decide how learning should be structured.

  An experienced professional may need substantial guidance when encountering an unfamiliar technology or theory. A university student may be ready to investigate a problem independently. The design decision therefore depends on the learner population, the content and the learning need.

notes: |
  Narration (7:00-8:30):
  The document translates Knowles' principles into explicit design implications.

  First, adults need to understand why a piece of learning matters before investing time. The recommendation is to open every microcredential with a sector-specific problem statement rather than a boilerplate objectives slide.

  Second, adult self-concept supports responsibility for learning decisions. The recommended default is choice in pace, location and order.

  Third, adult experience should be acknowledged and used. Existing practice, peer discussion and case interrogation should be central to the session rather than treated as a warm-up.

  The document takes a clear position on the conventional multi-day classroom format. It argues that this model can fail on five of Knowles' six principles at the same time: control, experience, task orientation, readiness and intrinsic motivation.

  Its conclusion is not that in-person learning has no value. The criticism is directed at the routine use of a multi-day, single-venue format when the learner's context and the purpose of the training would be better served by shorter, flexible or work-embedded alternatives.

left:

notes: |
  Narration (13:00-15:00):
  The section concludes with a six-question design checklist. Apply it to one existing module or proposed microcredential.

  Begin with the operational problem. Check the degree of genuine learner control. Identify where prior experience is used. Ask whether the learning can be accessed at the moment of need. Examine whether the structure follows workplace tasks or academic subjects. Finally, test whether the credential supports a meaningful professional identity.

  These questions provide the bridge from adult-learning theory to the practical design choices developed in the remainder of the methodology.

  Source: Section 2.2, design summary: andragogy checklist for every microcredential.
