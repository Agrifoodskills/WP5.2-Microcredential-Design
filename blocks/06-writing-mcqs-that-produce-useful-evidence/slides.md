subtitle: For learning or for mastery, flawed questions fail
notes: |
  Narration (0:00-0:45):
  An MCQ is not useful simply because it has a question and four possible answers. Its value depends on what a correct response actually tells us.

  A flawed formative item gives learners misleading feedback. A flawed mastery item may pass someone who lacks the competency or fail someone who holds it. In either case, the question has failed to produce useful evidence.

  Source: Appendix C, “Why Assessment Design Matters for Verifiable Credentials”.

--- slide
id: s05-02
layout: Comparison
title: For learning or evidence of mastery?
left_head: Assessment for learning
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Reveals gaps and misconceptions
    - Directs feedback and further practice
    - Can explain why each option is right or wrong
    - Supports another attempt
right_head: Assessment of mastery
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Supports progression or credential decisions
    - Must provide defensible evidence
    - Requires secure and appropriate conditions
    - Contributes to a claim of competence
notes: |
  Narration (0:45-2:00):
  The purpose changes how an MCQ result is used.

  In assessment for learning, the item helps identify a gap or misconception. Feedback can explain the reasoning and direct the learner towards another attempt. In assessment of mastery, the result supports a more consequential decision about progression, recognition or competence.

  But the standard of item construction does not change. Ambiguity is not helpful feedback. A clue that gives away the answer is not evidence of mastery. Both purposes require the item to test the intended capability rather than reading technique or test-wiseness.

  Source: Sections 7.2 and 7.5.1; Appendix C.

--- slide
id: s05-03
layout: Comparison
title: Two ways an MCQ can lie
left_head: Irrelevant difficulty
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Confusing or unnecessarily complex wording
    - Ambiguous options
    - Reading burden unrelated to the competency
    - A competent learner may answer incorrectly
right_head: Irrelevant ease
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Structural clues reveal the key
    - Weak distractors can be dismissed immediately
    - Test-taking strategies replace subject knowledge
    - A learner without the competency may answer correctly
notes: |
  Narration (2:00-3:15):
  A flawed item can distort the evidence in either direction.

  Construct-irrelevant difficulty makes an item harder for reasons unrelated to the capability being assessed. The learner may understand the subject but fail because the language, structure or options are confusing.

  Construct-irrelevant ease allows the answer to be found without the required knowledge or judgement. The learner succeeds by spotting patterns, eliminating implausible distractors or following an unintended clue.

  Good item writing removes both distortions. Difficulty should come from the cognitive task—not from defective construction.

  Source: Appendix C, “Why Assessment Design Matters for Verifiable Credentials”.

--- slide
id: s05-04
layout: Full
title: The anatomy of a single-best-answer item
full:
  type: ol
  items:
    - text: "**Stem — the scenario**"
      items:
        - Places the learner in a realistic context and supplies the information needed
    - text: "**Lead-in — the question**"
      items:
        - Asks one focused, closed question that can be answered before viewing the options
    - text: "**Option set — the possible answers**"
      items:
        - Contains one correct or best answer and three or four plausible distractors
notes: |
  Narration (3:15-4:30):
  A single-best-answer item has three connected components.

  The stem creates the scenario. The lead-in asks the question that follows from it. The option set contains the key—the correct or best answer—and plausible distractors.

  Each component has a different job. The stem supplies context without irrelevant detail. The lead-in focuses the required decision. The options test whether the learner can distinguish the best answer from credible alternatives.

  If any one component is weak, the evidence from the complete item is weakened.

  Source: Appendix C, “The Single-Best-Answer Format”.

--- slide
id: s05-05
layout: Full
title: Start with the claim—not the question
full:
  type: ol
  items:
    - Identify the competency and its EQF level
    - "Select the relevant EQF dimension: knowledge, skills, or responsibility and autonomy"
    - "Define the cognitive task: identify, apply, analyse, recommend, critique or evaluate"
    - Write one precise testing point before constructing the item
notes: |
  Narration (4:30-5:45):
  Do not begin by asking, “What question could I write about this topic?” Begin with the claim the item must support.

  Identify the competency, its EQF level and the dimension being assessed. Then define the cognitive task. At EQF levels four and five this may involve identifying, selecting or applying a procedure. At levels six and seven it may require analysis, synthesis or recommendation. At level eight it may require expert evaluation in a novel or contested situation.

  Finally, write the testing point in one sentence. This keeps the stem, lead-in and options focused on the same capability. The single-best-answer format can remain the same while the scenario, autonomy and cognitive demand change.

  Source: Appendix C, “What Should Be Assessed?” and “Connecting Items to Credential Specifications”.

title: The stem creates the decision context
head1: Role
card1:
  type: p
  text: Place the learner in a recognisable role consistent with the intended EQF level.
head2: Situation
card2:
  type: p
  text: Describe a realistic production, workplace or professional context.
head3: Evidence
card3:
  type: p
  text: Include the findings or information needed to make the required decision.
head4: Relevance
card4:
  type: p
  text: Remove window dressing, red herrings and detail that does not affect the answer.
notes: |
  Narration (5:45-7:00):
  A useful stem does more than introduce a topic. It creates the context in which the competency has to be applied.

  Open with a recognisable role that reflects the learner's expected responsibility and autonomy. Describe a realistic situation and provide the observations, results or history needed for the decision. Present the information in a logical order.

  Then remove anything that does not affect the answer. Extra detail is not automatically authenticity. If it increases reading load without changing the professional judgement, it creates irrelevant difficulty.

  Source: Appendix C, “Learner Profiles and Scenario Anchoring” and the item-writer checklist.

--- slide
id: s05-07
layout: Comparison
title: A good lead-in asks the learner to think
left_head: "Weaker: select from the list"
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - “Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate action?”
    - The answer lives in the options
    - Elimination and structural clues become more useful
    - The learner may choose without first generating an answer
right_head: "Stronger: answer the question"
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - “What should the operative do immediately?”
    - The answer must come from the learner's knowledge
    - The options confirm or challenge that answer
    - The lead-in passes the cover-the-options test
notes: |
  Narration (7:00-8:45):
  Consider a poultry-processing scenario. An operative notices that the oven temperature has fallen below the validated critical limit.

  The weaker lead-in asks, “Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate action?” This directs attention towards selecting from the options. The stronger version asks, “What should the operative do immediately?” A knowledgeable learner can generate “stop production, isolate the affected product and notify the supervisor” before seeing the choices.

  This is the cover-the-options rule: hide the options and ask whether a learner who holds the competency could answer from the stem and lead-in alone. Direct interrogatives—what, why, when, how, where or who—usually pass this test more naturally.

  Selective wording is sometimes appropriate for genuine classification or selection from a defined set, but it should not be the automatic default.

  Source: Appendix C, “The Cover-the-Options Rule” and “Direct Interrogatives and Why They Matter”.

--- slide
id: s05-08
layout: Comparison
title: Options must answer one question
left_head: "Flawed: mixed dimensions"
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**Lead-in:** What is most important regarding aflatoxin contamination of stored maize?"
    - Aflatoxin is produced by *Aspergillus* species
    - Grain moisture should be below 14%
    - Aflatoxin B1 is the most toxic form
    - Contamination can be reduced by rapid drying
right_head: "Revised: management practices"
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**Lead-in:** What is the single most important storage practice to reduce aflatoxin accumulation?"
    - Maintain grain moisture below 14%
    - Apply fungicide to grain at intake
    - Store grain in opaque bags
    - Turn the grain pile weekly
notes: |
  Narration (8:45-10:30):
  Options must be homogeneous: they should be the same type of thing and answerable on one dimension.

  In the flawed aflatoxin item, some options are factual statements and others are management recommendations. There is no single basis on which they can be compared.

  The revised item places a grain-store manager in context and asks for the most important storage practice to reduce aflatoxin accumulation. Every option is now a management practice. The key is maintaining grain moisture below fourteen per cent, while the distractors are superficially possible actions that do not provide the required control.

  Write the key first. Then create distractors from genuine misconceptions, incomplete applications or contextually inappropriate actions. A distractor that nobody could believe does not contribute useful evidence.

  Source: Appendix C, “Homogeneous Options” and “Connecting Items to Credential Specifications”.

--- slide
id: s05-09
layout: Comparison
title: Remove flaws before learners see the item
left_head: Creates irrelevant difficulty
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Long or unnecessarily complex options
    - Vague terms such as *usually* or *often*
    - Negatively phrased or exception lead-ins
    - Options with inconsistent grammar or structure
right_head: Reveals the correct answer
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - The key is longer or more qualified
    - Words from the stem reappear only in the key
    - Distractors use implausible absolute terms
    - Options form exhaustive or converging patterns
notes: |
  Narration (10:30-12:45):
  Technical flaws fall into the same two categories introduced earlier.

  Long options, vague frequency words, negative lead-ins and inconsistent grammatical forms add difficulty without testing more of the competency. The learner spends effort decoding the item rather than making the intended judgement.

  Other flaws make the item easier. The correct answer may stand out because it is longer or more carefully qualified. A key word may be repeated from the stem. Distractors may contain “always,” “never,” “all” or “none,” making them easy to reject. Patterns across the options may allow a test-wise learner to reconstruct the key.

  Review the item as a set, not as four independent sentences. The options should be comparable in length and form, free from cues, and genuinely plausible to someone who lacks the specific competency.

  Source: Appendix C, “Technical Item Flaws” and the item-writer checklist.

id: s05-10
layout: Cards
title: Audit and repair one MCQ
head1: Claim
card1:
  type: p
  text: What competency, EQF level and cognitive task should this item evidence?
head2: Prompt
card2:
  type: p
  text: Does the stem provide a relevant context, and does the lead-in pass the cover-the-options test?
head3: Options
card3:
  type: p
  text: Are the key and distractors homogeneous, plausible, parallel and free from clues?
head4: Evidence
card4:
  type: p
  text: Would a correct answer demonstrate the intended capability—or merely successful test-taking?
notes: "Choose one MCQ that you already use or have recently written.\n\nStart with the claim. Write down the competency, EQF level and precise cognitive task. Then cover the options. Can a knowledgeable learner answer the lead-in from the scenario alone? If not, revise the stem or make the question more direct.\n\nExamine the complete option set. Check that every option answers the same question, follows the same grammatical form and is credible to someone with an incomplete understanding. Remove length cues, repeated words, absolute distractors and unintended patterns.\n\nFinally, return to the evidence. If a learner answers correctly, what can you now claim? If the answer is only that they navigated the structure of the item, revise it again.\n\nThe guide created as part of workpackage 5.1 has many examples of removing different types of item writing flaws.\_"
