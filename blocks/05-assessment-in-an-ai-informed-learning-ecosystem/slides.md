  Narration (0:00-0:45):
  A completed task, a polished submission or a passing result is not automatically proof of professional capability. Assessment is the evidential bridge between what learners have experienced and what we can justifiably claim they can do.

title: Assessment for learning or evidence of mastery?
left_head: Developmental purposes
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**Assessment for learning:** identifies gaps and misconceptions, then directs feedback"
    - "**Assessment as learning:** develops self-monitoring, reflection and judgement"
right_head: Mastery purpose
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**Assessment of mastery:** supports progression, credentialing and claims of competence"
    - The strength of the evidence must be proportionate to the claim
notes: |
  Narration (0:45-2:15):
  Assessment can serve different purposes, and those purposes should not be confused.

  Assessment for learning is developmental. It reveals gaps or misconceptions so that teaching, practice and feedback can respond. Assessment as learning also supports development, but places more responsibility with the learner by strengthening self-monitoring and judgement.

  Assessment of mastery is different. It supports a decision about progression, recognition or competence. The evidence therefore has to be strong enough for the claim being made.

  The format alone does not determine the purpose. A well-designed selected-response task can assess applied judgement, while an uncritical workplace sign-off may show little more than attendance.

  Source: Section 7.2.

--- slide
id: s04-03
layout: Full
title: Assessment, evaluation and credentialing are different
full:
  type: ul
  items:
    - "**Assessment:** produces evidence about an individual learner"
    - "**Evaluation:** examines the quality and effectiveness of the learning provision"
    - "**Credentialing:** packages evidence into a recognised claim"
notes: |
  Narration (2:15-3:15):
  These terms describe related but distinct processes.

  Assessment asks what an individual learner can demonstrate. Evaluation asks whether the programme, module or learning experience is effective. Credentialing packages suitable evidence into a recognised claim, such as a badge, certificate or qualification.

  A learner result may contribute to programme evaluation, and assessment evidence may support a credential. But a satisfaction survey is not evidence of competence, and completing a course is not by itself a credential-worthy capability claim.

  Source: Section 7.1.

title: AI changes evidential value
left_head: Weak standalone evidence
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Unsupervised product-only work
    - An unauthenticated essay or reflection
    - A polished artefact without evidence of process or ownership
right_head: Stronger assessment design
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Secure or supervised conditions
    - Oral defence and authentication
    - Observed authentic performance
    - Process tracking and triangulation
notes: |
  Narration (3:15-4:45):
  Generative AI does not make assessment impossible. It changes the evidential value of some familiar products.

  An unsupervised essay or polished artefact may still support learning, but on its own it may provide weak evidence of authorship, reasoning or independent capability. The response is not simply to prohibit AI. It is to strengthen the relationship between the claim and the evidence.

  Secure conditions can help where independent recall or judgement matters. Oral defence can test ownership and reasoning. Observation can capture authentic performance. Process records and multiple sources of evidence can make the conclusion more defensible.

  Source: Section 7.3.

layout: Cards
title: Choose the method by the evidence needed
head1: Applied judgement
card1:
  type: p
  text: Use secure selected-response or structured response to test decisions, prioritisation and applied knowledge.
head2: Ownership and reasoning
card2:
  type: p
  text: Use a viva or oral defence when the learner must explain, justify and respond to challenge.
head3: Standardised performance
card3:
  type: p
  text: Use simulation or scenario-based assessment when comparable conditions and decisions matter.
head4: Authentic performance
card4:
  type: p
  text: Use workplace observation or an authenticated portfolio to evidence capability in context.
notes: |
  Narration (4:45-6:30):
  Choose an assessment method because it can produce the evidence the claim requires—not because it is familiar or easy to administer.

  Selected-response and structured-response tasks can test applied knowledge, decisions and prioritisation. A viva or oral defence is particularly useful when ownership, reasoning and synthesis need to be probed. Simulations allow performance to be assessed under standardised conditions. Workplace observation and authenticated portfolios can provide strong evidence of performance in context.

  There is no automatic hierarchy in which one format is always more valid than another. The question is whether the method, task and conditions reveal the intended capability.

  Source: Section 7.5.

id: s04-06
layout: Cards
title: Assess AI use through QUSC
head1: Question
card1:
  type: p
  text: Is the output true? Test for inaccuracy, fabrication and unsupported claims.
head2: Use
card2:
  type: p
  text: "Use AI effectively: choose tools and prompts well, and know when not to use it."
head3: Scrutinise
card3:
  type: p
  text: Examine the tool, data, context, provenance, bias and relevant professional norms.
head4: Communicate
card4:
  type: p
  text: Disclose how AI was used, why it was used and what it contributed.
notes: |
  Narration (6:30-8:15):
  Where AI use is part of professional practice, assessment should examine critical use rather than relying only on prohibition. QUSC provides four dimensions.

  Question means treating an AI output as a claim to be checked, not an answer to be trusted. Use includes choosing an appropriate tool and prompt, but also informed abstention when AI is unsuitable. Scrutinise means examining provenance, data, context, bias and the norms of the sector. Communicate means disclosing the role AI played and distinguishing its contribution from the learner's own judgement.

  QUSC allows AI-mediated work to become assessable professional practice: the product matters, but so do the learner's choices, critique and accountability.

  Source: Sections 2.3.6, 7.3 and 7.5.7.

id: s04-07
layout: Comparison
title: Every defensible assessment constrains
left_head: Assessment format
left:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Selected response
    - Structured response
    - Simulation
    - Workplace observation
    - Portfolio
right_head: Where the constraint sits
right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Options, lead-in and single-best-answer logic
    - Task wording, scope and rubric
    - Brief, time and performance criteria
    - Defined task and observation criteria
    - Required artefacts, mapping and authentication
notes: |
  Narration (8:15-9:45):
  Every defensible assessment constrains what counts as evidence. Formats differ mainly in where that constraint sits.

  In selected-response assessment, the options, lead-in and single-best-answer logic create the boundary. In a structured response, the task wording, scope and rubric do that work. A simulation uses a brief, a time limit and performance criteria. Workplace assessment defines the task and what will be observed. A portfolio specifies the required artefacts, their relationship to outcomes and how they will be authenticated.

  Constraint is not the enemy of authenticity. It makes the standard visible and the judgement more consistent.

  Source: Sections 7.5 and 7.6.

right:
  type: ul
  items:
    - Judgement and synthesis
    - Adaptation to unfamiliar contexts
    - Leadership and greater autonomy
    - Defence of decisions
    - Work with complexity and uncertainty
notes: |
  Narration (9:45-11:15):
  The assessment format does not have to change simply because the EQF level changes. The demand, autonomy and standard do.

  At levels three to five, assessment may focus on reliable performance in defined tasks, applied understanding, safe execution and supervised autonomy in relatively predictable contexts.

  At levels six to eight, the same broad format can require synthesis, adaptation, leadership, defence of decisions and work with uncertainty. A scenario question, simulation or oral assessment can operate at different levels if its complexity, independence and performance standard are appropriately designed.

--- slide
id: s04-09
layout: Full
title: A result is not yet a credential claim
full:
  type: ul
  items:
    - The competency or outcome claimed, including its EQF level
    - The assessment method and the conditions under which it was completed
    - The performance standard and the learner's result
    - The strength and type of evidence supporting the judgement
    - The assessor, date and period of validity
    - Whether AI was prohibited, permitted or required—and how its use was assessed
notes: |
  Narration (11:15-12:45):
  A score becomes credential-relevant only when we can interpret what it represents.

  A defensible assertion records the capability or outcome being claimed, its level, the method and conditions of assessment, the standard applied and the result achieved. It also records the strength of the supporting evidence, who made the judgement, when it was made and how long it remains valid.

  In an AI-informed environment, the status of AI use is also material: was it prohibited, permitted or required, and was the quality of that use itself assessed? This metadata preserves the meaning of the result when it is used beyond the immediate course.

  Source: Section 7.8.

--- slide
id: s04-10
layout: Cards
title: Audit one assessment
head1: Claim
card1:
  type: p
  text: Which capability or outcome—and which EQF level—is this assessment intended to support?
head2: Evidence
card2:
  type: p
  text: What does the chosen method genuinely allow the learner to demonstrate?
head3: Conditions
card3:
  type: p
  text: Where are the constraints, how is work authenticated, and what is the AI status?
head4: Confidence
card4:
  type: p
  text: Is the evidence proportionate, sufficiently triangulated and defensible?
notes: |
  Narration (12:45-15:00):
  Choose one assessment you already use and test it through these four questions.

  First, state the claim precisely: what capability or outcome, and at what level? Second, identify what the method genuinely allows the learner to demonstrate. Third, make the conditions visible: the constraints, authentication arrangements and status of AI use. Finally, decide how much confidence the resulting evidence deserves.

  If the evidence cannot support the claim, there are two honest responses: narrow the claim or strengthen the assessment design. That may mean changing the conditions, adding an oral defence, observing performance or combining more than one source of evidence.

  Session 5 moves from this assessment architecture to the detailed construction of multiple-choice questions: stems, lead-ins, options and common flaws.

  Pause the presentation while you record the four decisions.

  Source: Section 7.9.
